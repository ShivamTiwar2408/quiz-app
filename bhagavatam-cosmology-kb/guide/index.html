Reconciling Bhāgavatam cosmology with modern science

I am grateful to be here with all of you. From my travels I know you have a very good educational culture here and you study śāstra seriously, so let me tell you first how I ran into this problem myself, and then let us work through it — the milk ocean, the moon, the fourteen planetary systems, and what to do with a doubt you cannot settle today.

The one idea everything hangs off

A book has to be read in the light of the question it is answering. Science asks how nature works. Scripture asks how to work through nature to God. Those are two different questions, so of course the answers look different. Once I saw that, I could be devotionally faithful and intellectually honest at the same time — and I did not have to give up either one.

the problem, first hand

§0 How I ran into this problem

Since childhood I had a lot of interest in concepts — in understanding concepts through words. And naturally, because we live in a world of science and technology, I studied science. I read the biographies of scientists. I was very much attracted to the rational approach of science, where through evidence and through logic we come to appreciate things.

I was born in a brāhmaṇa family. We could do the religious rituals, but I was not really into religion so much. My focus was more on my own intellectual growth. I had read the Bhagavad-gītā in my childhood and memorised some verses from it to please my parents — but I never saw it as a book of wisdom.

So when I was introduced to Kṛṣṇa consciousness, what attracted me was primarily the rational approach. I found the logical presentation of the Gītā very, very appealing. The idea of the soul, even the idea of God — there was some rational evidence available for these things. That is what brought me forward. And along with that I experienced some personal transformation through the practice of bhakti. I felt it was adding a lot of meaning and purpose to my life.

Then I started studying the Bhāgavatam. Overall I found the Gītā a very rational book. But when I came to the Bhāgavatam, and especially Bhāgavatam cosmology — I did not study the cosmology directly myself at first, it was some comments about the moon and the moon mission and things like that — those caused a good amount of intellectual tension for me.

The question that came to me: until now I had tried to understand everything with my intelligence, and in one sense my intelligence had been my path to Kṛṣṇa. Now what the Bhāgavatam was saying just did not seem to be understandable through the intelligence. So did I have to give up my intelligence?

In philosophy circles this question comes up all the time: can faith and reason go together, or do they go in different directions? Faith on one side, reason — logic, rationality — on the other. For me it was not like that at all. Reason had been my path to faith. Not reason cancelling itself, but reason bringing me to faith. So when this particular section came up, it was quite a challenge. How do I make sense of the whole thing?

What I found is that there is much more subtlety and sophistication in how these things are to be understood than I had assumed. Apart from the Bhāgavatam itself, I went backwards in the tradition and studied Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura and his approach to the Bhāgavatam. He wrote a famous essay on the Bhāgavatam, which was his first devotional publication. Then he wrote a book called Kṛṣṇa-saṁhitā — a bit of a controversial book, but it has a very interesting approach in it. And I spoke with Śrīla Prabhupāda's scientist disciples, and I understood that there is so much more depth to what is being discussed here.

Since then I have tried to gain as clear an understanding as possible, and to share that understanding. So: can science and spirituality go together? The biggest challenge for me was in this area. And of the many principles that help, the most important one is the one I want to start with.

the master key

§1 The one idea: two different questions

Science, like every book, has to be understood in the light of the question it is answering. What is that question? Science answers how nature works.

When Newton saw the apple falling — some people say he felt the apple falling on his head — his question was: what made the apple fall? And although he believed in God, he was not interested in the answer “God made the apple fall.” He wanted to know the mechanism by which things worked. That is how he came up with the idea of gravity.

Now consider scripture. Scripture is not answering the question of how nature works. It is answering how to work through nature to God. Parīkṣit Mahārāja is seven days away from his death. By the time he is hearing the Fifth Canto of the Bhāgavatam, probably three or four days remain at the most. He is not interested in getting a PhD in cosmology. He is not interested in cosmology for cosmology's sake. He wants to understand nature in order to transcend nature. We live in the material world — so how do I gain an understanding of this world such that I can go beyond it and attain Kṛṣṇa?

The toy that stopped working

A child is playing with an electrical toy and it stops working. The child starts crying. What is the problem? “My toy is not working. I cannot play with my toy.” Ask the parent, and the parent will check and say: the battery got discharged. Ask a technician, and the technician will say: we have to replace the motor, the moving part has got spoiled. Same situation, three answers — and all three are correct. Which answer you get depends on which question you are asking.

So this is the fundamental point: science and scripture are primarily answering different questions. They may answer other things along the way, but their primary business is different. That understanding helped me make sense of things in a much more balanced way, where I could be both devotionally faithful and intellectually honest. That is what I want to discuss — how we can be rational and faithful at the same time.

Science asks

How does nature work? What is the mechanism? Give me a natural explanation for a natural phenomenon.

Scripture asks

How do I work through nature to God? I have limited time in this body. What actually matters?

what matters

§2 Why a devotional book contains cosmology at all

The first question I was asked was a fair one. The Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam is known as a devotional text, full of bhakti, meant to thicken our devotion — and yet it contains these extensive descriptions of cosmology. How important is that for a devotee to study?

See, the Bhāgavatam is primarily for one purpose: to remember Kṛṣṇa. That is what Parīkṣit Mahārāja wants to do. So the importance of the cosmology has to be seen from that ultimate purpose.

And the Bhāgavatam says that remembrance of Kṛṣṇa can happen in two ways. If I am here in this world and God is beyond this world, then either I can turn away from the world and remember God, or I can turn towards the world and through the world remember God.

Two words for these two directions. The first is God's transcendence — God exists beyond nature. The second is God's immanence — God exists in nature, so by looking at nature I can remember God. The Bhāgavatam describes both. In the Bhagavad-gītā too we have the virāṭ-rūpa, the universal form: Kṛṣṇa gives Arjuna the vision of how God can be seen in this world.

Put it another way. We live in the material world and we presently have material senses, so we cannot see God directly. What is the solution? God is beyond nature — how do we reach that God? It is solved in two ways.

One: God in nature. God descends into this world. These are the avatāras, and much of the Bhāgavatam is avatāra-kathā — starting from the Third Canto, on through the Fourth, to Nṛsiṁhadeva in the Seventh Canto, and Kṛṣṇa Himself in the Tenth. God enters into nature.

Two: nature in God. Nature exists within God. This is the other vision, and it is why the cosmology is there at all.

what matters

§3 The three places the Bhāgavatam shows nature inside God

For this second vision — nature in God — the Bhāgavatam has three main sections.

SectionWhat it describesWhat it is doing
Cantos 1–2The description of the virāṭ-rūpa, the universal form of the Lord.The universe presented as the body of God, so that looking at any part of it is looking at Him.
Canto 3The sāṅkhya description — Kapiladeva's teachings on the elements and how matter is composed.Analysis of matter, given by an incarnation of God to His own mother, ending in devotion.
Canto 5The cosmology — the brahmāṇḍa, the universal egg. The planets, the islands, the dimensions.The layout of the whole material world, so that we can locate ourselves and go beyond it.

So in these three ways we can look at nature and connect nature with God. And here is the thing to notice. The purpose of the Bhāgavatam, even when it is studying nature, is not simply to study nature. Read the cosmology chapters of the Fifth Canto and see what actually fills the pages. There is a description of this planet, these dimensions — but eventually where does the focus go? On this planet the people are worshipping Kṛṣṇa. On that planet they are worshipping this form of the Lord. On this one they are offering these prayers.

So when we look at the universe, what do we see? We see, as revealed in the Bhāgavatam, that everywhere in the universe there is dharma and there is devotion — and that inspires us to practise devotion. The purpose of the cosmology is to enhance our devotion.

Think of a devotee getting a chance to go to America. There are certain things such a person may want to see. But a devotee will also think: I want to go to the Kṛṣṇa temples over there. Where are the temples? And when we look at the map of America, we look for where the temples are, where I can go and take darśana. So the geography is interesting to us because these are the places where we can enhance our Kṛṣṇa consciousness. What applies to geography applies to cosmology.

So the Bhāgavatam is offering multiple ways to enhance our Kṛṣṇa consciousness. One is that we directly hear the avatāra-kathā, where the Lord has descended and we remember His pastimes. The other is where we see how the manifestations of nature can be connected with Kṛṣṇa.

an old argument

§4 This argument did not start with modern science

The next question was the obvious one. The Bhāgavatam's description of the cosmos seems different from what is taught in modern schools and universities. How do we approach that difference?

First thing to see: this is not a new problem. Even within the Indian tradition there were different schools of cosmology. I will not go into much detail, but among the many ways cosmology was studied within the broad Indian tradition, one was Bhāgavatam cosmology and another is what is called jyotiṣa cosmology — the cosmology of the jyotiṣa śāstras, which is what we use when we use astrology.

Now look at our ācāryas. Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura has commented on the Bhāgavatam. But Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura has also analysed, for example, the horoscope of Kṛṣṇa and the horoscope of Caitanya Mahāprabhu — and all horoscope study is done using jyotiṣa śāstra.

Between these two there is a huge difference. Jyotiṣa cosmology and Bhāgavatam cosmology are very different from each other. And Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura himself is not worried about it. When he is writing his commentary on the Fifth Canto, where the Bhāgavatam's cosmology is described, he does not stop to say “oh, this is different from the jyotiṣa cosmology.” Why not? Because he understands that there are different ways of looking at the universe.

Bhāgavatam cosmology

Looks at the universe primarily for the remembrance of Kṛṣṇa. A devotional cosmology. Its purpose is to transcend the world.

Jyotiṣa cosmology

Has many purposes, one of them maṅgala and muhūrta — what is auspicious, what is inauspicious. A functional cosmology. Its purpose is to function in the world, not to transcend it.

So within the Indian tradition itself there were different answers to these questions, because different questions were being asked. And the point I want to make is that Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura did not even consider this controversy something that had to be addressed, let alone resolved.

Why not? Because it is understood that the universe itself is acintya — inconceivable — just as Kṛṣṇa is acintya. And acintya does not mean that we can never understand it, so we should not even try. It means the universe is more complicated than our capacity to understand it with any single model.

Much of modern science today is similar to jyotiṣa cosmology in its purpose. On my Spiritual Scientist website I have written many articles on this and given many facts, which we can discuss separately if you want. In jyotiṣa cosmology there are descriptions of the distances between the various planets, between the earth and the sun, and they are very similar to what is found in modern science — and they were written long, long before modern science came about. So that is a valid body of knowledge.

Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura did not hesitate to use jyotiṣa cosmology for practical purposes such as determining horoscopes. Similarly, when we have to use modern cosmology for practical purposes, we do not have to hesitate. We are simply following our ācāryas, understanding that the universe is more complicated than any one way of looking at it.

the signature idea

§5 What the instrument decides

Let me explain this from another angle. When we look at something from a different scale of observation — with a different driving question, through a different instrument — we get different answers. Here is the case I keep coming back to.

Take chalk, which is white. Take coal, which is black. Powder both of them. Now we have a white powder and a black powder. Mix the two together, and what do we get? A grey powder.

Now look at that same powder under a microscope. To our eyes it appeared grey. Under the microscope it appears completely different. We will not see any grey particle at all. We will see white granules and black granules.

So is it white, or is it black? Is it white and black, or is it grey? Well — it depends on the level of perception. It depends on the instrument of perception.

Bhāgavatam cosmology is given primarily from a devotional perspective. The Bhāgavatam is giving a celestial vision — a vision from the perspective of a higher, more evolved soul. That is why these two accounts do not match.

And is that mismatch a serious matter of concern? No. We understand that different domains of work require different kinds of vision, and we focus on the vision required for functioning at our own level. The level of perception changes the nature of the observation.

The same thing happens with a face. Look at somebody and that person may be a beauty queen — the skin looks so smooth. Look at the same skin under a microscope and it is quite different. It does not look attractive at all. All kinds of what seem like hills and valleys and tracts and rocks and pointed things. Different level of perception, different observation. So it is understandable that there are contradictions.
And here is the practical rule I take from this. If the contradictions did not trouble our ācāryas — if they did not trouble Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura — then we should not get troubled by them either.
the two domains

§6 Matter, and what matters

Then I was asked: whenever there does seem to be a conflict between scripture and science, what principle can we use to address it? There are a few foundational things to get straight first.

Scripture has many branches of knowledge, but scripture ultimately focuses on spirituality. So let me put it like this. Science is the study of matter. Scripture is the study of what matters. It is not the study of matter; it is the study of what matters.

The Bhāgavatam: I am going to die in seven days — so what matters now? My kingdom does not matter. My family does not matter. The Gītā: Arjuna says if I fight I will have to kill my own relatives, I will have to kill Bhīṣma and Droṇa, and I don't want to do that, that is important to me. But if I do not fight, then those on the other side will kill my family, and that is unacceptable to me. So what really matters in life?

Scripture contains descriptions of matter, but scripture is not primarily focused on the study of matter. And science is amazing in its study of matter, but science really does not answer the ultimate questions about the meaning and purpose of life. They are different domains of knowledge. As long as we understand that, we can address the differences properly.

One caution before we go further. Scripture, rightly understood, is the word of God. But that does not mean that my understanding of scripture, or your understanding of scripture, is the word of God. Scripture is often more complicated than what we think it is.
the practical method

§7 Three possibilities when they contradict

If science and scripture appear contradictory, broadly there are three possibilities. Not one — three. And most arguments go wrong because people pick one and forget the other two.

PossibilityWhat it means
Science is incorrectScientific theories keep changing. Science settles around a particular understanding and that understanding may change with time. About a hundred, hundred and fifty years ago scientists started following Nietzsche, who said that God is dead. Then evolution came along, and more and more people started believing that. But now — it is not that scientists have started believing in God, but there is a lot more openness. For example, if you go to analytical philosophy, it has become strongly theistic, based on pure logic and analysis.
Science is incompleteIt is perceiving reality, but only up to a particular level. If you actually encounter honest scientists, no honest scientist will say that God does not exist, or that the soul does not exist. All they can say is: at the present level of study we have not found conclusive evidence for the existence of the soul. That is a pretty honest statement, isn't it?
Different scalesThere are different levels and different scales of perception — the chalk and the coal again. We need to look at which explanation applies at which point.

So we do not have to dismiss science for the sake of scripture, and we do not have to dismiss scripture for the sake of science. We can take a nuanced approach in which what science gives can be given its due respect.

“We are not against science and the knowing spirit of scientists. We are against their atheism.”

Śrīla Prabhupāda, talking with his disciples on Venice Beach

When science starts making the claim that God does not exist, that is when science is actually going beyond the range of science. As I said, there is no scientific proof for the non-existence of God.

What has actually changed. I would not say scientists in general have become open to the idea of the soul and God. What has definitely happened is that the monopoly on truth that science claimed to have has decreased. Scientists have become far more open to recognising that no one model explains all of reality — and therefore that there could be much to learn from different models.
how nature works

§8 A good method, and where it overreaches

Let me explain how science functions, because the honest version of it is not the enemy of anything, and it is worth defending before we say where the problem starts.

Science studies nature. That approach is called naturalism. But how does science study nature? It studies natural phenomena, and it looks for natural explanations for natural phenomena. This is called methodological naturalism — as a method, that is the rule. From our language's point of view you can say “natural” is more or less the same as “material.”

So Newton was not interested in God as an explanation. He wanted to find out the mechanism. That is how he gave the law of gravity. Of course he accepted that the law of gravity is ultimately created by God, but that was not the focus of his study.

And science has good reasons for working this way. This is not a wrong method at all.

Think of the black plague in Europe. When such terrible diseases came, suppose scientists at that time had accepted the idea that these people had been possessed by an evil ghost, and the church had to do some exorcism to drive the ghosts out. Sometimes the priests who did the exorcism also got infected and died. Now, if science had believed a supernatural explanation like a ghost, we would never have discovered germs. We would never have come up with medicines. Science would not have advanced.

So the idea is: when we look for natural explanations for natural phenomena, science advances. Once we start looking for supernatural explanations, the advancement of science stops. In that sense science is being very honest — we are looking for natural explanations for natural phenomena. And some scientists acknowledge exactly this: this is the methodology we use. Do ghosts exist? Does God exist? Maybe they do, maybe they don't — but science does not study them, because they are not natural phenomena. They are supernatural.

Where the overreach happens

But then something happened. From methodological naturalism, science started moving towards metaphysical naturalism — where it started claiming that because we can explain how nature works, therefore God does not exist.

That is the step that does not follow. Methodological naturalism is not a problem at all. Looking for a natural explanation for a natural phenomenon is perfectly fine. Claiming that a natural explanation proves there is nothing else is a different claim altogether — and it is not a scientific one.
Āyurveda does exactly the same thing, and nobody objects. If somebody gets a disease, what does an Āyurvedic physician do? Look for the explanation in terms of an imbalance of kapha, vāta and pitta. That is a material problem, and we are looking for a material cause. An Āyurvedic doctor might be a theist, might believe in Viṣṇu — but that doctor is not going to say “you got the disease, no need for me to check your pulse, no need for me to give you medicines, just chant Viṣṇu's name and you will be cured.” No. Traditional registered Āyurvedic physicians who were devotees would understand that the medicine works by the mercy of Viṣṇu — and they were still not going to reject or replace medicines for the sake of worship.

So this idea — that we look for a natural explanation for a natural phenomenon — is valid. There is nothing wrong with it. But is it complete? Is that the only explanation available? That is the question. And the Vedic understanding is that it is a correct explanation but not a complete explanation.

Why? Because this world is pūrṇa — complete — and this world has come from the Supreme Lord, who is also pūrṇa. This world being complete means the world can be explained in terms of the world. Most of the phenomena in the world can be explained in terms of the world. That is the way Kṛṣṇa has arranged it.

But notice which question that answers. Science can answer: how does rain occur? How do objects fall? Science does not answer: why do rains occur at all? Why do clouds exist at all? Why does the world exist at all? This is why I said the study of matter and the study of what matters. Science does not get into the why question so much. And so it is incomplete.
how nature works

§9 The map is not the territory

Here is the clearest way I know to say what science is and is not. Suppose India is a territory, and we have a map of that territory. The map is very useful — it helps us navigate the territory. But the map is not the territory.

If reality is the territory, then what science offers us is a scientific model of reality. And that model is very useful. Gravity, Newton's laws of motion — they offered a particular model of reality which was called the clockwork universe: the universe works like a clock. That is one scientific model.

Now for many people, because the model works so well, they started thinking that the model itself is the reality. See how that happens. When you first start using a map you are quite sceptical — will this really work? The map says go straight along this road and you will come to a bridge. You come to a bridge. It says go further and you will come to a hill. You come to the hill, go round and round it, go further, and it says you will come to a river. You come to a river. The more we follow the map, the more our faith in the map increases.

But say we are following the map to go and meet a friend. We follow it, we arrive at the friend's house, we get out, we knock on the door, the friend opens the door and welcomes us. And then we look at the map and say: “There is no friend in the map. I cannot see you here. Therefore you do not exist.”

That would be ridiculous, wouldn't it? The map is valuable. The map is a tool for reality. We should not reduce the reality to the map. Some people started reducing reality to the scientific model. Let me show you what I mean by that, with two things everybody in this room has already experienced.

There is no loveometer

Consider one of the biggest realities there is: love. Nowadays relationships are so unstable, people are so insecure. Say a boy proposes to a girl: please marry me. And the girl says, wait a minute — why do you want to marry me? “Because I love you very much.” Okay, let us see. The girl takes out a loveometer. I will place it on your heart and let us see how much you love me.

With all our scientific advancement, we cannot have anything like a loveometer. Now — is love a reality? We have all experienced it. If our mothers did not love us, we would not have survived the first few months of our life. Love is one of the biggest realities in the world.

There is no painometer

Similarly, consider pain. Much of medical science exists to remove pain. But there is no such thing as a painometer. You can have a thermometer to measure temperature. If somebody has a fracture, the doctor can estimate — okay, your bone is this much misaligned, your bone is broken this much, you must be in a lot of pain. But how much is the pain? There is no mathematical scale to measure it.

So here is the conclusion. There are many realities in the world that are very material but not mathematical, and they cannot be approached by science. Science offers us a model of reality. For some time many scientists and many scientifically influenced people believed the model was the reality. Now we are understanding: it is the model, it is not the reality. And if this model explains some things, there could be another model that explains other things.
a hard case

§10 The ocean of milk

Then came a very fair question. The Bhāgavatam is full of realities that are sometimes very difficult to comprehend or digest. Take the ocean of milk. How do we really understand that? Is there any rational way?

Yes. The idea of a milk ocean can seem quite unbelievable to us. So let us look at why it seems unbelievable, because the reason is an assumption, and the assumption has a name.

Uniformitarianism. Within science there is a school of thought which holds that all of reality is uniform — that the entire universe, or multiverse, or whatever there is, is similar in the sense that it all follows the same laws of nature. When we say laws of nature here we primarily mean the laws of physics.

Without that assumption science will not work at all. Consider the Andromeda galaxy or some other distant object. How far is it from us? If we had to go there, how long would it take? If the basic laws of physics were not the same there, science would not be able to function at all.

And how does science get most of its knowledge? Largely from what is called the principle of induction. Induction means: I observe something happening once. I observe it twice, three times, four times. When I have observed it n times, I assume — I induce — that it will happen the next hundred times too. We have observed that the laws of physics work on the earth, in various parts of the earth and in the vicinity of the earth, and therefore science assumes that all of reality will be like that.

Now, is that assumption proven? Certainly not.

Science's own counter-example: the extremophiles

Take the question of life on other planets. Some scientists say there is no life on other planets. But how do they know? They assume that all life will be like the life on earth: it will require water, it will require heat within a particular range, it will require oxygen. And when we ask whether some other planet could have life, what we look for is whether those conditions are there.

Are those the only conditions under which life is possible? Scientists themselves have discovered something called extremophiles. “-phile” means to love, and “extremo” means extreme, out of the ordinary — so these are living beings that live in extreme conditions. Deep in the ocean bed, scientists have found organisms which breathe in nitrogen dioxide, which is toxic for us. Deep inside the earth's crust we have found bacteria and microbes which live at temperatures that would burn human beings.

These are examples within our own observation. The assumption that all life has to be like the life we know is not necessarily a valid assumption — and it was science itself that showed this.

So yes: in the observed universe that we have, a milk ocean does not seem reasonable. But it seems unreasonable because we are extrapolating from our observation. And is that extrapolation valid? It rests on the assumption that all of reality has to be like the reality we have right now.

In archaeology and in history there is a principle worth memorising. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Say it again: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Another point to add. We understand that there are higher planets, and that elements exist there in a different way. Is the food there the way we eat food? We don't really know. If the bodies of the devas are made of subtle elements, then their food will be of subtler elements. So when we say there is a milk ocean in the higher planets, is that milk literally like the milk we get in a bag or a bottle and drink? Maybe it is, maybe it isn't. We don't know. Milk is valuable and scarce over here.

Where I would leave it. We do not have to immediately accept it — that may require a leap of faith some of us are not ready to take right now. But if we are just open-minded, we do not have to reject that possibility as impossible either. If we reject it outright, then it is we who are being presumptuous — it is we who are refusing to be open-minded enough to consider that there may be more in the universe than what we think is possible.
the hardest case

§11 The moon question, in four parts

Then the question everybody is waiting for. Every country is sending astronauts to the moon. How does a Bhāgavatam reader adjust the statement that a human cannot go to the moon? This is one of the toughest questions that people who come to bhakti face. Let me break it into four distinct parts.

Part one — what the Bhāgavatam actually says

The Bhāgavatam does not explicitly say that man cannot go to the moon. What it says — what the cosmology says — is that Candraloka is a higher planet, and a higher planet requires a qualification to get into.

And within that, Śrīla Prabhupāda himself, if you study the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, says there are three ways to get to heaven:

  1. By worshipping the devas — we go to their abodes.
  2. By yogic means — we can transport ourselves.
  3. By mechanical means — mechanical craft, spacecraft, mechanical devices.

So he does keep the possibility open that by mechanical devices we can go there. And we have examples of demons — Rāvaṇa, or Hiraṇyakaśipu, or Bali Mahārāja — who try to attack the heavens and are able to reach the heavens. If puṇya is the qualification, they do not necessarily have the puṇya, and they are reaching heaven anyway.

So the Bhāgavatam's statement is not as categorical as it is made out to be. It is not talking so much about possibility. It is talking about adhikāra — qualification. It is not saying we cannot go to the moon. It is saying that a particular set of qualifications is needed — and Prabhupāda says that sometimes mechanical spacecraft is also possible.
the hardest case · part two

§12 Part two — laws stretched, not broken

A natural follow-up came immediately: if puṇya is what is required for getting to heaven, then how do the demons get to heaven?

The answer described in scripture is this. The laws of nature cannot be broken, but they can be stretched. They cannot be violated, but they can be stretched. What does stretched mean? That sometimes some people can acquire some force by which they can do certain things. Rāvaṇa may not have had the puṇya, but he performed tapasya and got certain powers, by which he could rise and attack the heavens. So temporarily he was able to do that.

So it is possible that while the laws of nature do exist, they are not as rigid and inflexible as we imagine. In the same way we understand that sometimes people who commit pāpa also flourish for some time — because that is how nature allows a certain amount of room, a certain latitude, for people to act.

the hardest case · parts three and four

§13 Parts three and four — many statements, and what “access” means

Part three — Prabhupāda made several different statements

If we look at Śrīla Prabhupāda's statements — and there is an entire video on my YouTube channel explaining this elaborately with the explicit quotes, I do not want to make this session more technical than it already is, so I will not go through all the references — he makes multiple kinds of statements about the moon issue.

The statementWhat it says
“They didn't go at all”The most famous one, and the one usually attributed to him.
“Maybe they were diverted”Perhaps they were diverted to some other planet — the residents there are quite sophisticated and did not want them, just as Indra did not allow certain people to come to heaven, so they were diverted elsewhere.
“They went, but did not access it”He gives a slightly simplistic example which is worth elaborating: if somebody arrives on the earth in the middle of the Sahara desert, looks around, sees nothing, goes back and reports “there is no life on earth” — that is what happened. They went to Candra and did not see anything, did not see life forms. That means they did not access Candra.
“What did they get?”They went there, they spent so much money, and all they got was some dust particles and some samples.
“Why not rise in consciousness?”So much effort was made to rise to the heavens, to rise to Candra — then why not make some effort to raise our consciousness? In the introduction to the Bhagavad-gītā he says exactly this: just as you want to rise to the higher planets, make at least some of that effort to rise to a higher consciousness.
So we cannot reduce Prabhupāda's position to one statement. “This is what Prabhupāda said” — about a topic on which he said several different things — is not an accurate way to represent him.

Part four — access is a level of reality, not a location

Now let us look at what “they did not access the planet” means. Sometimes we extend our idea of material geography to higher realities.

For example, we all know the visualisation of the universe on the cover of the Bhāgavatam. This is the material world, this is the spiritual world, and there is the river in between; if you cross over the river you can go to the spiritual world. That is one way of depicting it. But it is not that this world is like India and the spiritual world is like China — just cross over a particular boundary and you get there. The spiritual world does not exist at another place. It exists at another level of reality. And going there requires not just physical travel; it requires actually getting to a higher level of consciousness.

So even if somebody physically goes to the moon planet, that does not necessarily mean that they have accessed Candraloka as a higher planet — because it exists at a different level of reality. In our tradition the word for this is adhikāra.

The mainframe. Nowadays we have very decentralised systems, but in the past a company had one big mainframe computer, and multiple people worked on that same machine. If a clerk is working on it, the clerk has access to only some files. The clerk's manager has access to many files. A branch head may have access to much more. The owner may have access to the whole thing. They may all be touching the same computer and pressing the same keys — but they are not actually accessing the same computer. It depends on their access.

Many times it is described in scripture that when a yajña is performed successfully, the devas come and accept the offering — but nobody sees the devas. Why? Because if the yajña has not been done so well, they are not obliged to reveal their visibility. They are seen only very rarely, when they come.

So somebody may physically go to what we consider the moon planet, and yet not access the higher reality there. And this idea of access also applies to Vṛndāvana. When we go to Vṛndāvana, it is not just a physical location — there is a spiritual reality there, and we do not access that spiritual reality so easily.

So what Prabhupāda is essentially saying is: even if they went to the moon physically, they did not access the moon as a higher planet.

What I would not do with this question

We do not have to reject what modern science and its achievements are, for the sake of faith in scripture. Prabhupāda has given multiple possibilities. Whichever possibility makes sense to us, we accept that, and we move forward in life.

Prabhupāda did not start the International Society for Moon Conspiracy Exposure.

This was not a major issue for him

This was one thing he spoke about, and his teachings are many, many things. We do not have to reduce faith in Prabhupāda or faith in śāstra to one particular teaching, one particular point. It is not that when we are going to die, Kṛṣṇa is going to ask us: do you believe in the moon mission? No? Then you cannot go back. It is not like that.

Central and additional. There are central teachings in the Bhāgavatam and in our tradition, and there are additional teachings. I would not say the additional ones are less important, but they are not central. So we do not have to torture ourselves unnecessarily because we cannot reconcile something. And we do not have to make this a faith test for other people.
what matters

§14 Literal, non-literal, and spiritual

The next question came from a change that anybody who has been around for three decades has watched happen. Thirty-five years ago, an audience would not challenge the authority of scripture, and would not ask intellectual questions. There was a class, and everyone accepted it, because it was coming from scripture — Kṛṣṇa is wonderful, Kṛṣṇa can do anything. Now the generation has changed. There is more intellect in the room. Gen Z, the youth brigade — very intelligent, with more reasoning power than the previous generation had at the same age.

So the question is: if they have difficulty accepting Bhāgavatam cosmology in totality, can they still accept Kṛṣṇa consciousness? Can they still render devotional service? Can they still establish faith in bhakti and make progress?

There are different approaches to this. Let me start with Jaiva Dharma, the book Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura wrote. In it he explains that when we look at scripture, there are several ways of looking at it: a literal way, a non-literal way, and a transcendental or spiritual way. This applies to the kathā in scripture, and it applies to the descriptions of cosmology and chronology and things like that.

One clarification, because the words are borrowed. He describes people at the beginning, intermediate and advanced levels — in Bengali, kaniṣṭha, madhyama and so on. But he is not using those words in the sense of levels of spiritual advancement. Beginning, intermediate and advanced can be measured in multiple different perspectives. So his analysis here is not the same as the kaniṣṭha/madhyama of Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu or elsewhere.

What he is saying is that some people take scripture at the literal level, and that is all they can understand. For them it is binary: either this is literally true, or it is literally wrong. And if it is literally true, it is the word of God — and anybody who refuses to have faith in it is faithless, is lost, cannot be a devotee.

Now yes, we want to take scripture seriously. Take śāstra seriously. But here is what I want you to sit with: sometimes taking śāstra seriously means taking it not literally. Sometimes we take it literally in order to take it seriously. Sometimes we take it non-literally in order to take it seriously.

An example from scripture arguing with scripture. The Īśopaniṣad speaks of those who are the killers of the soul. But one major teaching of the second chapter of the Bhagavad-gītā is that the soul cannot be killed. So does taking scripture seriously mean that the second chapter is wrong? Or that the Īśopaniṣad is wrong? No. Taking it seriously means looking at the context — and here, where the reference to killing the soul comes, it is non-literal.
So the point is this. A non-literal approach is not just an intellectually acceptable approach. On some occasions it is the intellectually respectable approach.

And so, if some things in scripture are not immediately understandable to the rational mind, that may mean the whole understanding of scripture is not available to us at the literal level. Maybe there is more that has to be explored.

the practical answer

§15 Suspending judgment

A person may not have faith in everything. But just because they do not have faith in one thing, they do not have to reject everything. There is a third position available, and it is the honest one.

Suspend judgment. It does not mean “I accept it.” And it does not mean “I reject it.” It means: this is something which does not make sense to me right now. I have to be intellectually honest with myself. But there is a lot in scripture that does make sense to me, and there are a lot of ways in which what I have learned has added value to my life. Therefore I will continue my study of scripture, and about these things which I am unable to understand, I will suspend judgment. Maybe in the future I will understand.

This is a mature approach towards learning any field, especially any sophisticated, complicated field. We do not necessarily understand everything right away. We suspend judgment.

Nobody abandons quantum physics

Take quantum physics. The whole field of quantum studies is very complicated. A famous quantum physicist said that if you think you have understood quantum physics, that is the proof that you have not understood quantum physics.

So it is very complicated. Does that mean that because it is complicated we stop studying it? That does not make sense. I feel this desk as a solid object. I feel this phone as a solid object. But according to quantum physics, all that exists is wave-particles, and those waves collapse when you observe them. I am putting it very simply. Even Einstein had difficulty accepting quantum physics. He said, “I would like to believe that the moon continues to exist if I am not looking at it.” Obviously the moon exists whether I look at it or not — but according to quantum physics, no, it is not that simple.

So many things in quantum physics do not make sense. But we do not reject the whole of quantum physics and abandon it. We suspend judgment about it. Anybody who is serious about learning any subject understands that there will be aspects of the subject they do not understand.

And this cuts both ways. Suspending judgment is from the student's side — but it is also from the teacher's side. Just because a particular student cannot accept a particular point, that does not mean the teacher has to label the student as faithless, abandon the student, reject the student. Both sides need to give each other intellectual space.
what matters

§16 Room for doubts, exactly as we make room for desires

Let me make one fundamental philosophical point here, because it settles the whole thing for me.

We have a subtle body, and in the subtle body there is the mind and there is the intelligence. Through the mind, desires come. Through the intelligence, doubts come. Both are coming from the same subtle body.

Now — if people have desires, we give them space for their desires. It is not that we say everybody has to be a pure devotee, and if you are not a pure devotee you cannot even enter the temple. Our temples would be empty like that. So we give people space to fulfil their desires.

Of course there are boundaries. If somebody says I want to eat meat every day, then it will be difficult for them to practise bhakti. Chanting will benefit them, but they will not grow very fast. So there are boundaries to the desires — but the boundaries are not so rigid that if you have one impure desire you cannot practise bhakti at all.

So the question answers itself. We give people space for their desires. Why not give people space for their doubts? Different people have different kinds of desires. In exactly the same way, different people have different kinds of doubts. Just as we do not make desires non-negotiable in the practice of bhakti, we do not have to make doubts non-negotiable. People can practise bhakti at that level.

Scripture itself makes room for the intellectual

Śāstra actually gives a lot of space for doubting, and those who are analytical, those who are intellectual, need some space for analysis. That is why Caitanya Mahāprabhu, when He is speaking to Sanātana Gosvāmī, gives sixty-four explanations of the ātmārāma verse.

Now, one explanation is enough: even those who are self-satisfied are attracted to the Lord, therefore the Lord's attraction is transcendental. That is enough. So why are sixty-four explanations required? Because Sanātana Gosvāmī is an intellectual. And Caitanya Mahāprabhu — the Lord who descended as a brāhmaṇa — is an intellectual too. They are relishing that intellectual rasa. So do not say “this is the only right meaning, and there is no need for any other meanings.” Śāstra gives space for intellectual exploration, and within that space a person can have doubts.

Where the line is. If somebody says “I doubt whether God exists,” “I doubt whether the soul exists” — these are non-negotiable truths. Such a person can still practise bhakti, but the bhakti will not be very substantial. Between that and doubts about cosmology there is a great deal of distance.

what matters

§18 How this actually feeds devotion

The last question before we opened the floor was the right one to end on. Beyond satisfying intellectual curiosity, how can studying Bhāgavatam cosmology actually enrich our devotion and deepen our faith in Kṛṣṇa? Two main ways.

One: bhakti turns out to be universal — literally

No matter how different the various parts of the universe seem to be, and some of them as described in the Bhāgavatam are far more opulent than our places — even in such places, people are practising bhakti.

See, we often think that horizontal improvement is what we want in life. If you are staying in a 1BHK house you want to go to a 3BHK house. If you are in a 3BHK you want your own bungalow. If you are in India you want to go to America. We want this horizontal improvement.

But the Bhāgavatam says: people who are materially far better off than us — even they are practising bhakti. Whichever part of the universe, however fantastic those beings may be, still they are practising bhakti. So bhakti is what is ultimately the goal. Seeing that universality of bhakti — universality meaning literally across the universe — can increase our conviction that this is what I should be practising too.

Two: the prayers themselves

The second aspect is that studying the specific prayers is itself a way to understand how Kṛṣṇa is actually all-attractive. When we study the prayers of particular characters, they are offering those prayers because they are appreciating Kṛṣṇa from their own individual perspective. Understanding that perspective is very helpful. That way we can enrich our own appreciation of Kṛṣṇa.

And notice what that does to the Fifth Canto. The chapters are not a gazetteer to be memorised. They are a record of how Kṛṣṇa looks from every vantage point in creation.

§22 Are the holy places portals?

Śivāṅgī Mātājī

You mentioned that Vṛndāvana is a cosmic reality, so we exist in interdimensional realities. My question is about the dhāmas — the holy places like Māyāpur and Vṛndāvana. That is a good point. I would say that is one level of explanation — it is just not the complete explanation.

§24 Where are the boundaries — and what is “one fourth”?

Sanātana Prabhu

We have learned that one fourth is the material world and three fourths is the spiritual world. So how can we define the boundaries? If you take a ship with unlimited fuel and we keep on moving and moving, where will we end? What are the boundaries of the two worlds? How do we assess the boundaries, the space, the dimensions?

This is exactly what I have been saying. The boundaries are not just physical. The boundaries are also at the level of consciousness. When we practise bhakti, we are trying to raise our consciousness.

It is described in the Ninth Canto of the Bhāgavatam that when Durvāsā Muni was chased by the Sudarśana cakra, he went to heaven — he even went to Vaikuṇṭha. But he could not stay in Vaikuṇṭha. He went there to please Viṣṇu, and then he came back from there.

I don't think that is literal, because the words used are ekapāda-vibhūti and tripāda-vibhūti. My understanding is that it is indicative — it is telling us that the spiritual world is far bigger than the material world.

But the tip of a hair is not a precise dimension at all. How much is the tip of a hair? Are we taking 1 mm as the tip of a hair? 1 cm? The point being made is that it is extremely small. That is the key point there.

§26 How everything “comes from” Kṛṣṇa

The last question of the session

Kṛṣṇa says that from Him comes everything. But it is also said that the jīvas were never created, and that the spiritual world is not created. How can we understand these things together? If everything came from Kṛṣṇa, and yet the jīva exists always —

There are statements in scripture, in spiritual texts, that are chronological, and there are statements that are philosophical — the technical word is ontological.

Chronological

Time sequencing. This came at this time, and that came after it. Chronology talks about precedence — what came before what.

Ontological

Describing the nature of reality. Ontological analysis talks about dependence — not that one came before the other, but that this depends on that.

So when it is said that the brahmajyoti comes from Kṛṣṇa — does that mean there was a time when Kṛṣṇa existed and the brahmajyoti did not, and at one particular point it started? No. The focus there is not precedence.

Kṛṣṇa says in the Gītā, in the tenth chapter, that everything comes from Him. If everything comes from Him, then why does He also say that the jīvas are eternal? If they are eternal, how can they come from Kṛṣṇa? This “coming from Kṛṣṇa” is not in terms of time. It is in terms of dependence. Kṛṣṇa is the primary, foundational reality, and everything depends on Kṛṣṇa. Everything comes from Kṛṣṇa not in a time-sequence sense, but in a dependence-of-existence sense: without Kṛṣṇa existing, nothing would exist.

That is one of the definitions of God — God is the existence that makes all existence possible.

Methodological naturalismThe working rule of science: look for natural explanations for natural phenomena. Valid, and productive.
Metaphysical naturalismThe further claim that nature is all there is — that a natural explanation proves nothing else exists. Not a scientific claim.
UniformitarianismThe assumption that all of reality follows the same laws of physics everywhere. Science needs it; it has never been proven.
InductionObserving something n times and concluding it will hold the next hundred times. Most of science's knowledge is built this way.
ExtremophileAn organism that lives in conditions we assumed no life could survive — nitrogen dioxide on the ocean bed, heat inside the earth's crust.
adhikāraQualification; access. The central word of the moon question.
CandralokaThe moon as a higher planetary system — a level of reality, not only a location.
puṇya · pāpaPious credit; sinful reaction.
tapasyaAusterity, performed to acquire power or purification. What Rāvaṇa used to stretch the laws.
yajñaSacrifice. Done well, the devas accept the offering — whether or not anybody sees them.
devaA demigod; a resident of the higher planets, with a body of subtler elements.
lokaA world, a planetary system, a plane of existence.
JambūdvīpaOne of the places of existence in the Vedic cosmos, described in the Fifth Canto.
dvīpa · maṇḍalaAn island or land-division of the cosmos; the disc-like arrangement they form.
dhāmaA holy abode — Vṛndāvana, Māyāpur — which is a spiritual reality and not only a location.
Bhauma VṛndāvanaThe Vṛndāvana on this earth, as distinct from Goloka Vṛndāvana in the spiritual world.
bāla-līlāThe Lord's childhood pastimes — which the ācāryas say happen here only.
sādhanaRegulated spiritual practice.
VaitaraṇīThe river said to be crossed on the way out of the material world; here read as a level of consciousness to be attained.
ekapāda-vibhūti · tripāda-vibhūtiThe “one-part” and “three-part” opulences — the source of the one-fourth / three-fourths figure, read as indicative rather than as a measurement.
jīvaThe individual living being, the soul — described as eternal, never created.
brahmajyotiThe spiritual effulgence emanating from the Lord.
Chronological / ontologicalStatements about what came before what, versus statements about what depends on what. Confusing the two creates most of the contradictions here.
kaniṣṭha · madhyamaBeginning and intermediate. In Jaiva Dharma used for levels of reading, not levels of spiritual advancement.
rasaRelish, flavour of relationship — including the intellectual relish that Sanātana Gosvāmī was given room for.
kathāNarration; the stories of scripture.
darśanaSeeing, and being seen by, the Deity.
kapha · vāta · pittaThe three Āyurvedic humours, whose imbalance is looked to as the material cause of disease.
