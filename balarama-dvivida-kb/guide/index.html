A chapter about a monster
is really a chapter about you

Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 10.67 — Lord Balarāma slays Dvivida

On the surface this chapter is thirty verses about a huge ape wrecking a coastline until Balarāma kills him. But that is not what it is about. The commentators hardly write about the fight at all. They write about one question: how did a servant of Lord Rāmacandra become a demon? The chapter answers it step by step, and each step has a cause you can point to. The fight is only the setting. The steps are what I want us to look at.

The one idea everything hangs off

Dvivida did not fall because he was weak. He fell because he was strong, and proud of it, and that pride made him slight the Lord's representative. Offending a devotee takes away your spiritual strength. Without that strength you cannot resist bad association. Bad association gives you anarthas. Anarthas bring more offences. That chain is the chapter, not the ape.

adhikāra the framing question

§0 What this chapter is for

Every chapter of the Bhāgavatam answers a question somebody actually asked. Here the person asking is Mahārāja Parīkṣit. He has seven days left to live, so he does not waste words. He has just heard two chapters that mention Balarāma, and he wants to hear more.

“I wish to hear further about Śrī Balarāma, the unlimited and immeasurable Supreme Lord, whose activities are all astounding. What else did He do?”

Parīkṣit's request · SB 10.67.1

So the purpose of the chapter is to glorify Balarāma. Śukadeva Gosvāmī does that, and I will end where I begin — with Balarāma's greatness. But notice how he does it. He tells the story of somebody falling down. And the commentators on this chapter — Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura, Jīva Gosvāmī — spend their words not on the fight but on one question: how did a servant of Rāmacandra become a demon?

Keep both of those in mind. The Lord's glory and the warning to us are the same story from two sides. We can only see how strong Balarāma is by seeing what He had to defeat. And what He had to defeat was once one of His own.

The chapter in one sentence: somebody who was eternally situated as an attendant of Lord Rāmacandra was allowed to fall — through pride, then offence to Lakṣmaṇa, then bad association, then unlimited anarthas — so that we would be able to see, step by step, what it costs to offend a great soul.
Read that way, the chapter is not about an ape at all. It is about the one danger that can undo even an advanced devotee: vaiṣṇava-aparādha. Nothing else in this chapter is difficult. That is.
krama sequence in the text

Chapters 65, 66 and 67 come one after the other, in order. If you know the two before this one, you will see why Parīkṣit asks what he asks, and why Balarāma is where He is when Dvivida finds Him.

kāla position in cosmic time

§2 Where we are in time — and why it matters

The first verses tell us three things about Dvivida: he was a friend of Narakāsura, he was Mainda's brother, and Sugrīva had instructed him. That last one moves the whole story. Sugrīva belongs to Rāmacandra's pastimes, and Dvivida dies during Kṛṣṇa's. So if those two appearances are far apart, Dvivida has been alive a very long time. The chapter's first fact about him turns out to be a fact about his lifespan.

The Bhāgavatam's clock is fixed. One day of Brahmā — a kalpa — has 1,000 four-yuga cycles. Those are divided among 14 Manus, so about 71 cycles each. We are in the reign of the seventh Manu, Vaivasvata Manu, son of Vivasvān the sun-god. Rāmacandra appeared in his 24th cycle. Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma appeared in his 28th. So they are four cycles apart — not in neighbouring yugas, as people often assume.

Cirañjīva: a living being given a very long life, who stays alive across yuga-cycles. Dvivida is one. So is Jāmbavān, who fights in Rāma's pastimes and again in Kṛṣṇa's. Jāmbavān's daughter Jāmbavatī became Kṛṣṇa's second wife, one of His principal queens. The same long life let Jāmbavān become the Lord's father-in-law and let Dvivida become the Lord's enemy. So a long life by itself decides nothing.
The Ikṣvāku line, briefly. Rāmacandra comes in the sun dynasty. The line runs Vivasvān → Vaivasvata Manu → Ikṣvāku, who brought that dynasty down to Earth and ruled from Ayodhyā. Rāma appeared in Ayodhyā and He left this world from Ayodhyā, entering the Sarayū river. Both ends of His pastime are in the same place.
Why I go through the arithmetic. Divide 1,000 cycles by 14 Manus and you get about 71. That one division turns “Rāma and Kṛṣṇa were both in Vaivasvata Manu's reign” into a gap you can measure. And it is the gap that makes Dvivida's long life remarkable instead of assumed. The numbers are not decoration. They are the proof.
janma origin and service

§3 Born to serve Rāma

Dvivida was not made to be a demon. He was made, on purpose, to help the Lord. How that happened is worth knowing.

The loophole Rāvaṇa left open

Start with Rāvaṇa's problem — or rather, everybody else's problem with Rāvaṇa. He had taken benedictions from Lord Brahmā, and those benedictions listed whole categories of beings who could not kill him. Demigods could not. Demons could not. He had gone through the list carefully, as anybody would, and covered everything that seemed worth covering.

With that protection he became unstoppable. He troubled this world as he liked, and then he was not satisfied with this world, so he went after the heavenly planets as well. There was a great fight with Indra, the king of the demigods — and Indra lost. The one who defeated him did not have that name before. He took the name Indrajit from that victory. Jit means conquered, defeated: the one who defeated Indra. Imagine losing a fight so badly that the winner names himself after you.

So the demigods had nowhere left to stand, and they went to Lord Viṣṇu. He agreed to come. And He gave them an instruction before He came — a very practical one. Go and produce children on Earth in the categories that Rāvaṇa's benedictions do not cover. Because when Rāvaṇa asked for his protection, he had not bothered to include animals. Why would he? What can an animal do to somebody who has just beaten the king of heaven?

That is where the vānaras come from. The monkey race in the Rāmāyaṇa is not a coincidence of the forest. Each one of them is a demigod's son, born in the one shape Rāvaṇa forgot to guard against. So when you read about a monkey army, understand what is standing in front of Laṅkā: the sons of the demigods, in the bodies of the only species that can touch him.

Among them, the Aśvinī-kumāras — the twin physicians of heaven — had two sons: Mainda and Dvivida. They grew up to be ministers in the court of the monkey king Sugrīva, and they fought as extremely powerful warriors. Their names come up again and again through the Rāmāyaṇa, right through to the great battle in the Uttara-kāṇḍa. They were not extras. They were front-line.

Remember this. The only reason Dvivida existed was to serve Rāmacandra. He is not a demon who happened to meet the Lord. He is a servant who was later given a demon's part. That is what makes his case worth studying.

Service in the search for Sītā

Now Sītā has been taken, and nobody knows where. Sugrīva sends his armies out in the four directions to look for her. Dvivida and Mainda are sent south, in a party of about thirty thousand vānaras — and Hanumān is in that same party. Every important name in this story is in one search party going in one direction.

The leader of it is Aṅgada. He is the son of Vāli — and there is a small, good story behind that. Vāli was Sugrīva's brother, and they had fought, and when Vāli was dying he asked Sugrīva for one thing: look after my son as your own son. Sugrīva did exactly that. He did not treat Aṅgada as the dead rival's boy. He raised him and made him his right-hand man, and now he puts him in charge of the southern search. Keep that in mind, because of what Aṅgada is about to say about Sugrīva.

They go south, and south, and south, all the way to the southern tip of India, and they find nothing. And there the party stops, and at the very edge of the land Aṅgada gives up. Look at how he reasons it out. We were given a job and we have failed. If we go back to Kiṣkindhā empty-handed, Sugrīva may kill us for it. So we are not going back. We will sit down here on the shore and fast until we die.

Think about what he is saying about the man who raised him as a son. That is what despair does to your judgement. And it spreads — many of the vānaras agreed with him and lay down on the sand beside him to die.

Hanumān did not. He said: this is the wrong way. We were given an instruction. We carry out the instruction. That is what a servant does — you do not sit down and die because it turned out to be difficult. But Aṅgada was the leader, and Aṅgada would not listen. So there they all lay, thirty thousand of the strongest beings alive, waiting to starve on a beach.

Sampāti, who could not fly

Now the scene widens, and the help comes from a direction nobody was looking in.

Up on the mountainside above that beach lived Sampāti — a great vulture, and the elder brother of Jaṭāyu, the same Jaṭāyu who had already given his life trying to stop Rāvaṇa from carrying Sītā away.

Sampāti could not fly, and here is how that happened. Long before any of this, the two brothers were young and strong and they decided to see how high they could go. They flew up and up, towards the sun, higher and higher, until the heat became unbearable. And Sampāti, being the elder brother, did what an elder brother does — he spread his own wings over the younger one to shield him from the sun. Jaṭāyu was protected. Sampāti's wings burned. He fell out of the sky, and from that day he never flew again.

For a vulture, not flying means more than not travelling. It means you cannot hunt. So he had been sitting on that mountainside, hungry, for a very long time.

And then he looks down and sees thirty thousand monkeys lying perfectly still on the sand below him. Put yourself in his position. He thinks one word: lunch.

So he starts making his way down the mountainside towards them — he cannot fly, so he is coming down slowly, on foot. And as he gets closer he begins to hear them talking. And what they are talking about is Jaṭāyu. He comes nearer, close enough to hear it properly, and he hears the whole story — how his younger brother fought Rāvaṇa alone, and how he died doing it.

And the vulture who came down that mountain to eat them wept for his brother instead. They performed the funeral rites for Jaṭāyu together, right there. Then they told him their problem: we cannot find her, and we have decided to die here.

And Sampāti gave them the one thing he still had. I cannot fly. But I can still see — about eight hundred miles. And he looked out across the water, and at that distance he found her: Sītā, sitting in the Aśoka grove in Laṅkā.

So a mission that was over is alive again, and now it has two parts: go and confirm that she is there, and tell her Rāma is coming to rescue her. Which raises the obvious question — who is going to cross eight hundred miles of open ocean?

See what saved that mission. It was not strength. Thirty thousand of the strongest beings alive were lying on a beach waiting to die. It was one person who would not give up an instruction, and one crippled bird who could still do one thing. The chapter is about to show us the opposite lesson from the same cast.

One of the five who stayed

Before Rāmacandra left this world, entering the Sarayū, He chose five of His devotees to stay behind: Hanumān, Jāmbavān, Vibhīṣaṇa, Mainda and Dvivida.

This is the fact the whole chapter turns on. The Lord Himself picked Dvivida to stay. So whatever happens next happens to somebody the Lord chose. If it can happen to him, then “could it happen to me?” is a real question.
bala how strong he was

§4 How strong he was

The scriptures do not just say Dvivida was strong. They give numbers, twice, from two different books. The reason to notice is that his strength is what his pride was made of, and his pride is the first link in the chain.

The jumping contest at the southern shore

So they are standing on that shore with the good news and the impossible problem. Sītā is a hundred yojanas away across open water — about eight hundred miles — and somebody has to get there, tell her Rāma is coming, and get back.

Aṅgada does the sensible thing. He puts the question to the whole assembly: who here can jump that far? And one by one they stand up and answer him, and Vālmīki writes the answers down, and each one is a bigger number than the last. It reads like a contest, because that is what it turns into.

Gaja stands up: ten yojanas, eighty miles. Gavākṣa: twenty, a hundred and sixty. Then Mainda: sixty yojanas — I am capable of four hundred and eighty miles. Then his brother Dvivida stands up, and listen to the difference in how he says it: “Mainda was not as strong as me. I can certainly jump seventy yojanas” — five hundred and sixty miles.

And then Hanumān, who has said nothing this whole time, answers last. A hundred yojanas — and back again. He is the only one who does not turn the answer into a boast, and he is the only one whose answer is actually enough.

Look at how they say it. Mainda states what he can do: “I am capable of 480 miles.” Dvivida cannot state his number without putting somebody underneath it — and the person he puts underneath it is his own brother. That is where strength quietly turns into pride in strength: bala becomes bala-abhimāna. Nothing has gone wrong yet, and he is still on the right side, still serving Rāma. But the seed of everything that ruins him is sitting there in that one sentence, in front of everybody, and nobody notices.

The second number: ten thousand elephants

By chapter 67 the Bhāgavatam states his strength directly. He went into the ocean and churned the water with his arms with the strength of ten thousand elephants, and flooded the coastal areas. He pulled up mountains and threw them. He tore down whole forests of śāla trees, which is a hardwood, by himself.

He is also on Kaṁsa's list. SB 10.2 describes Kaṁsa persecuting the Yadu kings, protected by Jarāsandha of Magadha, with the help of demons like Pralamba, Baka, Cāṇūra, Tṛṇāvarta, Aghāsura, Muṣṭika, Ariṣṭa, Dvivida, Pūtanā, Keśī, Dhenuka, Bāṇāsura and Narakāsura. So by that point in the text Dvivida is already counted among the enemies. Chapter 67 is not where he turns bad. It is where he finishes.
One thing to be clear about on that list. Not everybody on it was under orders. Dhenuka, for example, was not sent by Kaṁsa. He was simply living in the Tāla forest. But he was ready and willing to trouble the Yadu dynasty. So the category is “available for the demons' work”, not “sent”.
nitya-siddha the hard question

§5 Was he an eternal associate? The hard question

Here the chapter creates a real difficulty, and I don't want to hide it. The commentators say Mainda and Dvivida are eternally liberated, and that they are worshipped as attendant deities of Lord Rāmacandra. But souls in the Lord's abode do not fall down. So what happened?

What Jīva Gosvāmī says

In Bhakti-sandarbha — one of his six Sandarbhas — Jīva Gosvāmī says Mainda and Dvivida are worshipped as āvaraṇa-devatās. I did a lot of research to find out what that term means. It means “covered” or attendant deities. They are in the Lord's eternal abode as part of His party. They do not show the fullness of Viṣṇu themselves, but they attend on Him — so whoever worships Rāmacandra is worshipping them too.

In his commentary on this chapter he says it again: “the Mainda and Dvivida mentioned in this verse are persons endowed with śakti from the spiritual realm. Because they did not respect Lakṣmaṇa — like Jaya and Vijaya — they appeared as demons in order to show the result of committing offences.”

Somewhere else he makes a finer point. Some jīvas are given a portion of the śakti of the Lord's eternal associates, and they carry the same names, so that they can help Rāma or another avatāra when He comes to Earth. Those jīvas can become corrupted, because they are jīvas. Dvivida associated with Narakāsura, who hated the Lord. He was a mortal jīva mixed with the material elements, even though he had some of the eternal associate's power. So he could be affected.

So the fall was arranged by the Lord. It was not an accident of free will in the spiritual world. Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura says it plainly: “the Lord arranged their degradation to show the evil of the bad association that results from offending great personalities.” The parallel case is Jaya and Vijaya, the gatekeepers of Vaikuṇṭha, who took demoniac births after the four Kumāras cursed them.
Do not make a general rule out of this. The living beings in this material world were not once residents of Vaikuṇṭha who fell down from there. Jaya and Vijaya are an exception, arranged so that we would learn something, and so is Dvivida. Let me say it clearly: it is the exception, it is not the rule. Take the lesson. Don't take it as a theory of where you came from.
And Mainda? Somebody asked me what happened to the brother, and I had looked into that before I came. There is only one mention: he fell to the position of being Mainda, the brother of Dvivida, in this world. Both of them had been nitya-siddha, and a nitya-siddha does not fall unless the Lord wants it — so this fall was also by the Lord's will, like Jaya and Vijaya's. But Jaya and Vijaya's fall has a full description in the curse of the four Kumāras. For Mainda and Dvivida I am not aware of any Purāṇa that describes the circumstance. Not every gap has something to fill it.
aparādha the two offences

§6 The two offences

It took me a while to work out what the offence actually was. In the end it became clear, and what came out is that the offence is not the pride itself. Pride is the condition. The offence is what the pride produced. There are two of them, both against Lakṣmaṇa, and both are easy to read straight past in the Rāmāyaṇa.

Offence one — the introduction

Picture the scene properly, because it is a good one. This is the first meeting. Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa have come to Sugrīva looking for help, and Hanumān is the one making the introduction — he is standing over on the far right with his club in his hand. Sugrīva is there wearing the crown. And just behind Sugrīva, standing in attendance the way ministers do, are the two brothers, Dvivida and Mainda.

So everybody in that room is on the right side. Everybody there is about to spend the rest of the Rāmāyaṇa serving Rāma. And in the middle of the introduction, while Hanumān is presenting the two brothers to his king, Dvivida leans in and gives Sugrīva a piece of advice:

“Give your full, undivided attention to Rāma. The other fellow with Him — Lakṣmaṇa — is not so important.”

Dvivida's counsel to Sugrīva · the first offence

It is not abuse. It is a ranking. He sorts the Lord and the Lord's brother into important and not so important — and he does it while he is helping, in the middle of service.

He is not swearing at Lakṣmaṇa. He is not opposing anybody. He is being helpful — telling his king where to put his attention. He has simply sorted the Lord and the Lord's brother into two boxes: important and not so important. And he does it while he is standing in the middle of doing service.

Offence two — the judgment on the battlefield

The second one happens much later, in the middle of the great war at Laṅkā. Lakṣmaṇa is struck by the śakti weapon — a terrible weapon — and he goes down. He is lying on the battlefield in tremendous pain, unable to get up.

And Dvivida is there, watching it. And he is a warrior with the strength of ten thousand elephants, and he knows it. And he looks at the Lord's brother lying on the ground and he thinks — not says, thinks — “what kind of great hero is this?”

Nobody heard it. There was nothing to hear. He did not say a word to anybody. And that thought is the second offence, and it is counted exactly like the first one.

Neither offence was said to Lakṣmaṇa's face, and neither was an act of hostility. One was well-meant advice. The other was a private thought. Both counted. This is the most uncomfortable fact in the chapter and the one to carry with you: vaiṣṇava-aparādha does not need malice, or volume, or an audience.
Both offences come from the same root. He measured Lakṣmaṇa against himself. The first time Lakṣmaṇa comes out low on importance. The second time he comes out low on heroism. If you make your own strength the measuring stick, sooner or later a devotee will fall short of it. That is what pride does. It is not a feeling. It is a measuring stick pointed the wrong way.
guru-tattva why this person

§7 Why Lakṣmaṇa, of all people

People often shorten this warning to “don't offend Lakṣmaṇa,” and then the reply is easy: fine, but I'm not likely to meet him. Let me close that door straight away. Lakṣmaṇa matters here as an example of a category.

Guru-tattva: the principle of the Lord's representative — the one who introduces the Supreme Lord to others, gives instruction, and gives association. Lakṣmaṇa holds this position for Rāma, just as Balarāma does for Kṛṣṇa, and Nanda Mahārāja also. If you slight the representative you are slighting the very means by which anybody ever reaches the Lord.

So look at the shape of the offence. He pointed attention towards the Lord while running down the person who was making the introduction possible. He kept the object of devotion and threw away the channel. It sounds reasonable. It is fatal.

There is a neat symmetry here. Lakṣmaṇa is to Rāma what Balarāma is to Kṛṣṇa. Both are guru-tattva, and in our understanding both are the same personality. Dvivida offends the guru-tattva in Rāma's pastimes and is killed by the guru-tattva in Kṛṣṇa's. Across yugas, he is dealt with by the very principle he slighted.
The general point I want to insist on: it is not that “you're not Lakṣmaṇa, so I can mess with you.” The principle is vaiṣṇava-aparādha — offence to any devotee. It is not a light matter, and the consequences are just as serious. A person can lose his spiritual standing because of an offence.
anartha the steps, in order

§8 How the degradation worked

This is the chapter. Everything else is setting. Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura gives the whole sequence in one sentence, and it reads like a chain of causes because that is what it is:

“Dvivida was a minister of the great devotee Sugrīva and an associate of Lord Rāmacandra. He became corrupted by the bad association of Naraka, which caused anarthas to rise in him. This bad association was the reaction for an offence Dvivida had committed when he disrespected Lakṣmaṇa due to being proud of his strength.”

Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura · commentary on SB 10.67

Read the order carefully, because it is the opposite of what we assume. We think bad association causes the fall. Here the bad association is itself the punishment for an offence that came earlier. The offence came first. The corrupting friendship was the sentence.

Anartha: literally an un-thing — something unwanted, of no value, in the way. In this chapter the anarthas are not abstract. They are the fires, the fouling of the sages' sacrifices, the men and women shut into caves, the molesting of women from respectable families. That is what “anarthas rose in him” comes to.
Here is the part that should make you sit up. The chain has one voluntary entrance — the offence — and after that it runs by itself. Once your spiritual strength is reduced, resisting bad association is no longer a question of determination, because determination is the thing that got reduced. That is why our tradition treats vaiṣṇava-aparādha as more dangerous than ordinary sin. Ordinary sin damages you. An offence damages the very thing you would use to recover.
Where to step in. Look at the diagram and find the last step you actually control. It is not “bad association” — by then the result is more or less written. It is pride, and in particular the habit of measuring devotees against yourself. That is the practical lesson of the whole chapter.
saṅga the association that finished it

§9 Narakāsura: the association

Now let us look at the friend, because the friend has exactly the same disease — and an even better background. Narakāsura was not born a demon. He was not born as Narakāsura at all. The asura on the end of his name is something he earned.

He was born Naraka, and look at his parents. His mother was Bhūmi — the earth herself. His father was Lord Varāha, the boar incarnation of the Supreme Lord. There is no better parentage available anywhere in the universe. Divine on both sides.

And he became corrupted the same way Dvivida did — by bad association. That is the whole explanation. Somebody with the Lord Himself for a father ends up, by SB 10.59, as Narakāsura, holding sixteen thousand one hundred princesses locked in his prison. They appealed to Kṛṣṇa. Kṛṣṇa agreed to help them. And getting them out meant killing him, so Kṛṣṇa killed him and freed all sixteen thousand one hundred.

Notice the pattern repeating. Good people become bad people by bad association. Naraka was corrupted by association. Then Dvivida was corrupted by associating with Naraka. Corruption spreads. That is why association is treated as a first-order spiritual matter and not a social detail.

And that is the news that reaches Dvivida. His best friend is dead, and the one who killed him is the Supreme Lord. Now, a person with any spiritual strength left would hear that and understand what it means. But Dvivida has been offending, and he has been weakened, and he has been keeping this company for a long time. So he takes the only step he has left in him: He killed my best friend. I will get back at Him.

And he cannot fight Kṛṣṇa, so he decides to do the next thing — destroy the kingdom. Kṛṣṇa's kingdom is flourishing, prosperous, well protected, full of people who love Him. So that is what Dvivida sets out to wreck, piece by piece.

The motive matters for the diagnosis. Dvivida's rampage is not hunger and it is not madness. It is revenge — a grievance held on behalf of somebody he loved. Worth noticing, because revenge is the anartha that justifies itself best. It always feels like loyalty.
Ānarta the province he ravaged

§10 The rampage on Ānarta

He went straight for the area around Dvārakā — not some distant frontier, but the Lord's own neighbourhood. And now the Bhāgavatam slows down and lists what he did, one thing after another. I want to read the list out properly rather than summarise it, because the list is the teaching. This is what a chain of anarthas looks like when nobody stops it — not a feeling in somebody's heart, but burnt villages and ruined harvests and women who cannot go home.

  • He set fires. Not one fire — he burned cities, he burned villages, he burned the mines, he burned the cowherd villages where the cows and the cowherd families lived.
  • He pulled mountains up out of the ground and used them as tools to devastate the neighbouring kingdoms. And the verse tells us where he concentrated: the province of Ānarta — because that is where Lord Hari, the one who killed his friend, was living.
  • He waded into the ocean, and with the strength of ten thousand elephants he churned the water with his arms — just swept his arms through the sea — until the waves went over the shore and the whole coastline flooded.
  • He went to the āśramas of exalted sages and tore down their trees, and then he did something worse: he fouled their sacrificial fires with his own excrement and urine. These are men who have given their whole lives to that fire. He put filth in it. And in the paintings of this you can see what he wears around his waist — a belt of skulls.
  • He caught men and women and shut them into mountain caves, then rolled boulders across the mouths to seal them in. The Bhāgavatam's own comparison is exact and horrible: he did it “as a wasp imprisons smaller insects.” They are still in there while the chapter goes on. Balarāma lets them out at the end.
  • He could take an enormous form whenever he wanted, and with that he looted and destroyed the kingdom's crops — which means famine for people who had nothing to do with any of it.
  • And he polluted women from respectable families.

Read that list again and remember where it started: a warrior standing in a court saying, helpfully, that the Lord's brother is not so important. Nothing in between is a jump. Every step follows from the one before it.

A word on the word: Ānarta and anartha

They sound alike and they mean opposite things. Ānarta is a name — the province that holds Dvārakā (an island), the holy place Prabhāsa nearby, and Raivataka Mountain. Subordinate kings ruled it under Ugrasena. Anartha is the technical word for something unwanted that gets in the way.

I remember Śrīla Prabhupāda once giving a woman disciple the name Ānartā, and everybody was taken aback. It sounded like naming your child after one of the Kauravas — who would name their son Duryodhana? Then somebody remembered the province, and it was obvious. He had named her after the land where the Lord lived and ruled, not after the unwanted thing. One extra vowel.

līlā the scene he walked into

§11 Raivataka: the dance

And in the middle of all this destruction, something stops him. He hears singing — sweet singing, coming from Raivataka Mountain. So he goes to have a look.

And what he finds on that mountain is not an army and not a fortress. He finds the Lord enjoying Himself. Let the verse describe it, and notice as you read that every single detail in it is about to become a target.

There he saw Śrī Balarāma, the Lord of the Yādavas, adorned with a garland of lotuses and appearing most attractive in every limb. He was singing amidst a crowd of young women, and since He had drunk vāruṇī liquor, His eyes rolled as if He were intoxicated. His body shone brilliantly as He behaved like an elephant in mada.

avajñā contempt, one step at a time

§12 The provocation

Now watch what he does, step by step, because this is the part people read past. He has the strength of ten thousand elephants and he has come to take revenge on the Lord's family — and he does not attack. He heckles. And each thing he does is one notch worse than the thing before it.

  1. First he gets up into the branches and announces himself. He shakes the trees and he makes the sound kilakilā. Now, I don't know exactly what that sound is. It is a gorilla sound. Whatever it is, he wants to be noticed, and he is.
  2. And here is the reaction he was not expecting. The young women with Balarāma see this ape crashing about in the branches, and they laugh at him. They are young girls, they are fond of joking, and a gorilla making faces in a tree is funny. So they laugh.
  3. That is what sets him off — being laughed at. So now he goes after the girls. He makes strange movements with his eyebrows at them. He walks around in a ridiculous way in front of them. He comes right up and plants himself directly in front of them. And then he exposes himself and urinates in front of them — and the verse is careful to add: while Lord Balarāma is standing right there watching.
  4. Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura points out the detail that cuts deepest, and it is not any of the above. It is this: he disrespected Balarāma by not even glancing at Him. He would not look at Him. He is in the presence of the Supreme Lord and he does not consider Him worth turning his head for. And he is deliberately spoiling Balarāma's enjoyment with the gopīs — and Balarāma is not going to allow that.
  5. So Balarāma responds — and He throws a rock at him. Dvivida dodges it. And then he does something calculated: he goes for the pot of vāruṇī, the thing the Lord is enjoying, and grabs it.
  6. He smashes the pot. Then he starts pulling at the girls' clothing. And the whole time he is laughing at Balarāma and making fun of Him.
The detail worth the whole section. Balarāma's first response is a rock, not the club. The commentary says why: because of Dvivida's previous position as a devotee. Even in the middle of all this, the Lord's response is measured against who this person used to be. The weapon He picks says something about memory.
Read the escalation as a diagnosis. He is not fighting. He is trying to be noticed. Shaking trees, making faces, exposing himself, breaking the pot, pulling at clothes — that is somebody whose grievance needs an audience. And the one insult the commentator picks out is that he would not look at the Lord. What is being recorded here is contempt, not violence.

Only then does the text turn. Balarāma saw the ape's rude behaviour, and He thought of the disturbances he had created in the surrounding kingdoms — and He angrily took up His club and His plough, having decided to put His enemy to death. The verdict rests on the ruined province, not the ruined party.

yuddha the exchange

§13 The fight, blow by blow

Now the fight, and I want to go through it hit by hit, because the shape of it is the whole point. It escalates — but it escalates in two opposite directions at the same time. Dvivida keeps reaching for something bigger and keeps running out. Balarāma keeps putting weapons down until He has nothing in His hands at all.

It starts with a tree. Dvivida reaches over, pulls up a śāla tree — one-handed, and a śāla is a hardwood, like oak, not something you snap off a branch — and brings it down on Balarāma's head. And Balarāma does not dodge and does not stagger. He stands there like a mountain and catches the log in His hand.

Then He hits back, once, with the club Sunanda, on the ape's skull. And the blood comes pouring down over him, and the Bhāgavatam describes that sight in a way you would not expect for a demon — like a mountain beautified by streaks of red oxide. Even the wound is described as beautiful.

And Dvivida does not even notice the wound. He pulls up another tree. Balarāma breaks it into hundreds of pieces. Another tree. Broken into hundreds of pieces. And this goes on and on until — and this is a detail worth sitting with — the forest has no trees left in it. He tore up a whole forest, one trunk at a time, and every single one was destroyed in the air.

So he has no trees. He starts throwing stones, a rain of them, whatever the ground will give him. Balarāma smashes every one.

And now he has nothing left at all. So he clenches his fists — arms the size of palm trees — and starts beating the Lord's body with his bare hands, because there is nothing else in the world left to pick up.

And that is the moment Balarāma throws the club and the plough aside. His enemy has come down to bare hands, so the Lord puts down His weapons and meets him bare hands to bare hands. Then one hammering blow to the collarbone, and Dvivida goes down, vomiting blood, and he is dead.

And when he fell, Raivataka Mountain shook — the cliffs and the trees trembling, the verse says, like a boat tossed about at sea. And the demigods and the perfected mystics and the great sages called out “Victory to You! Obeisances to You! Excellent! Well done!” and showered flowers down on Him.

The śāla-tree connection. I asked where else śāla trees come up, and the answer is in the Rāmāyaṇa: Rāma pierced seven śāla trees with one arrow to show Sugrīva His power. Dvivida was standing in that court. Now he swings one of those trees at Rāma's own brother, and it is caught in the air. When the verse describes the mountain shaking it addresses Parīkṣit directly: “O tiger among the Kurus.”

And then Balarāma went and did the thing that is easy to skip over. He walked to the valleys and the caves, rolled the boulders away, and let all those men and women out — the ones who had been sealed in like insects in a wasp's nest. Then He went back to His capital, and the whole way there the people came out and chanted His glories.

How Jīva Gosvāmī tells the same fight, in Gopāla-campū

Gopāla-campū is Jīva Gosvāmī's book on Kṛṣṇa's pastimes. The first part covers the pastimes in Vṛndāvana and the second part those outside it. It is written as a conversation between two brāhmaṇas — Snigdha-kaṇṭha asks and Madhu-kaṇṭha answers.

His version says what the Bhāgavatam leaves unsaid: “Understanding that Dvivida was an associate of Rāma, Balarāma tolerated everything. When Dvivida showed impudence a thousand times, Balarāma killed him. Though He killed him, I think He was merciful — for He liberated him from his various offences.”

Two things arrive together in that sentence. The patience is counted — a thousand times. And the killing is called mercy.

mukti the outcome

§14 Killed, and so liberated

Now a point of doctrine that changes how you read every death in the Tenth Canto. It comes from SB 2.7, the chapter that lists the Lord's scheduled incarnations. SB 1.3 does the same thing with slightly different information. It is worth reading both.

All these demoniac personalities — Pralamba, Dhenuka, Baka, Keśī, Ariṣṭa, Cāṇūra, Muṣṭika, Kuvalayāpīḍa the elephant, Kaṁsa, Yavana, Narakāsura and Pauṇḍraka; great marshals like Śālva, Dvivida the monkey, Balvala, Dantavakra, the seven bulls, Śambara, Vidūratha and Rukmī; as also great warriors like Kāmboja, Matsya, Kuru, Sṛñjaya and Kekaya — would all fight vigorously, either with the Lord Hari directly or with Him under the names Baladeva, Arjuna, Bhīma and so on; and the demons, being thus killed, would attain either the impersonal brahmajyoti or His personal abode in the Vaikuṇṭha planets.

SB 2.7.34–35 · the scheduled incarnations

So the descent in §8 turns out to be a round trip. The soul who fell from his position as an attendant in the Lord's abode is put back there — by being killed by the Lord.

Let me head off the obvious conclusion right away. “So all I have to do is become a demon, get killed by Kṛṣṇa, and I have my ticket.” — We don't imitate. The mercy shown to Dvivida tells us something about the Lord's nature. It is not a method available to us. You cannot arrange to be an exception. That is what exception means.
Why Kṛṣṇa comes at all. Bhagavad-gītā 4.7–8 gives two reasons, and this chapter is a clear case of both. Yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati — whenever dharma declines and adharma rises, He comes. Paritrāṇāya sādhūnāṁ vināśāya ca duṣkṛtām — to protect the devotees, destroy the miscreants and re-establish dharma, age after age. And when the earth becomes too heavily burdened He may come Himself. Balarāma frees the villagers from the caves and kills the ape in the same afternoon.
You can see Balarāma's mercy in the shape of the fight. He throws a rock instead of the club, because of who Dvivida used to be. He tolerates a thousand impudences. He puts His weapons down and meets bare hands with bare hands. Then He kills him, and the killing is the liberation. It is all one gesture, and only the last part of it looks like violence.
kavaca how to use it

§15 Balarāma's four protections

Garga-saṁhitā has a kavaca — an armour prayer — with a large section on Balarāma. Its seventh verse does something nice. It matches each demon Balarāma killed with the internal enemy we are asking Him to protect us from. The pastime becomes a prayer, and the prayer becomes a diagnosis. The phrase at the centre of it, the one I put up on the screen in large letters, is dvivida-prahārī — the one who struck down Dvivida.

Where this comes from. At the moment of the killing the demigods glorified Balarāma as the slayer of Dvivida and showered flowers on Him. When we recite the kavaca we are joining that same glorification, thousands of years later and in the same words. That is how a chapter about an ape becomes something you can use on a Tuesday.
vāk-saṁyama a question I was asked

§16 Controlling the tongue

Somebody asked how to control the tongue. It is a fair question in a chapter where the first offence was something said. I gave four things, from the most structural to the most direct.

1 · Cultivate the mode of goodness

Bhagavad-gītā 17.15 defines austerity of speech: speak truthfully, and speak pleasingly. Both halves are the instruction.

The trap in following only half of it. “I just have to speak the truth, because I'm in the mode of goodness.” That is not the mode of goodness. Sattva includes the pleasingly. Truth used as a weapon has changed modes without changing content.

And you cannot call up goodness on demand. You cultivate it — by association, and by the habits that carry it: cleanliness, compassion, charity, truthfulness. Build the mode and the tongue follows.

2 · The tongue has two jobs

Tasting and vibrating. So take only kṛṣṇa-prasādam. And the reason is not that it is a rule. Prasādam is not simply food in the mode of goodness. It is prasādam, and that spiritualises the tongue. The purity gives you the ability to control it, rather than the control making you pure.

3 · Learn the mechanics of speech

One of my disciples has enrolled in professional training to become a certified teacher of non-violent communication. She teaches it among devotees as empathic communication, because a devotee wrote a book on empathic communication which she likes and learned from, and who mentors her. The root idea is: understand what your own needs are and take the steps to meet them; don't expect others to meet them. Because if they don't, you get frustrated, and frustration is what sets the tongue going. “You may bite your tongue, but you may say it in your mind anyway.”

Why this belongs in a Bhāgavatam class. Somebody says: it's nice to say in principle, but what about the details? Speak truthfully and pleasingly is the principle. There is a science to the details. It does not compete with the principle. It is how the principle gets carried out on a particular day with a particular person.

4 · Chanting

The best shelter, and the one that covers both jobs at once: the tongue only tastes and vibrates, and chanting the holy names uses both.

krodha a question I was asked

§17 Anger, and why bhakti is the cure

The sharpest question of the morning. Somebody said: I read the Gītā verse on anger and I understand it — once anger starts there is no stopping point, because you lose your mind, your intelligence and your memory. So I think, I should stop before it starts. But it starts.

That is Bhagavad-gītā 2.62–63 put as a practical problem instead of a doctrine. From anger comes complete delusion, from delusion bewilderment of memory, and from that the loss of intelligence — and then one falls down again into the material pool. The verse describes a system that switches off the very faculty you would use to stop it.

The fire, and where the comparison stops. Lust is like that too — lust burns like fire, and once a fire starts it only gets bigger and bigger. Anger is the same. Sometimes a person does press the pause button — what is going on here? what am I so angry about? what am I gaining? what is the real issue? But you cannot depend on that, because the faculty that asks the question is the first thing anger takes away.
And one clarification I want to make carefully. Is Dvivida a symbol of anger? Not a symbol of it, but he is very much moved by it. Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura, in Caitanya-śikṣāmṛta and Kṛṣṇa-saṁhitā — two of the hundred books he wrote — takes the demons Kṛṣṇa killed in Vṛndāvana as standing for the obstacles Kṛṣṇa removes on the path of bhakti, and a devotee has given a very skilled seminar in several parts going through them demon by demon on those lines. Dvivida is not one of them. He is killed outside Vṛndāvana, and by Balarāma. So this kavaca connection is not Bhaktivinoda's scheme. It is the simpler fact that anger drove Dvivida from the first verse to the last.

The proof case: Bhīma and Duryodhana

Here is the strongest evidence I know for this, and it is right at the end of the Mahābhārata. Bhīma and Duryodhana were both Balarāma's own students. He personally taught both of them how to fight with the club. They learned everything they knew from Him.

And at the end they fought each other with those clubs, and the duel went on for days and days, and neither of them could finish it. So Balarāma travelled all the way to where they were fighting, and He stood there and asked them to stop. Not ordered — asked. Your teacher is asking you. Please stop. And He explained it to them plainly, because He was the one person alive who could see it clearly: Bhīma is stronger, Duryodhana is more skilled. Neither of you can defeat the other. You are wasting your time.

They could not stop. Not would not — could not. They had learned their whole art from this teacher, and anger was in control. When anger controls you, you have got beans in your ears. You cannot hear good advice even from the wisest and dearest person in your life. Not even from Balarāma. That is the limit of willpower.

So what actually works

The same answer as for the tongue, and it is structural, not heroic. Anger is ignorance and passion mixed. You cannot fight your way out of a mode. You can only change modes. SB 1.2 gives the lever: worship of Viṣṇu raises a person to the quality of goodness. So give what you have — your possessions, your intelligence, your energy, your work and the results of your work — to Him, and come up out of passion into goodness. That is the real cure.

And then ask directly. Not self-improvement, but the kavaca from §15: may Lord Balarāma, who killed Dvivida, always protect me from anger. Please help me get over it. In one sentence — bhakti is the cure. Balarāma can take away the tendency itself. He has done it before, in public, to the strongest angry person in the chapter. And don't get angry at Balarāma.
āśraya how I finish

§18 Where I take a problem

We are reading this on Balarāma-pūrṇimā, and I want to finish where the chapter finishes — not with the ape but with the two brothers in Vṛndāvana, Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma. And with something practical to do about the anger and the pride and the offences we have just spent an hour going through.

Śrīla Prabhupāda was once out on a morning walk, and somebody asked him a very ordinary question: what do I do when there is an obstacle in front of me and I cannot get past it? Not a philosophical question. A practical one, the kind you actually have.

And his answer was as simple as it is possible to be. Go and stand in front of the deity — of Gaurāṅga, or of Kṛṣṇa-Balarāma, they are non-different, whichever deity you have — and say this: “Sir, I have a problem. Please help me.” That is all. And he will do the needful.

Two things he added, both important. Not because the Lord is an order-supplier — that is not the relationship. But because He is merciful to somebody who depends on Him the way a child depends on a parent. And: don't make it too much of your business to bother Him too many times. But when something is really needed, you know where to go, and He will help.
A confirmation from the room. A devotee here said that they pray exactly like that, and that it is true — not instantaneously, but it works. Which is a good place to stop. Kṛṣṇa is very kind and Balarāma is all-powerful. That is a good combination to have on your side.
pralaya appendix

§19 Appendix: the questions on time

The arithmetic in §2 started off a set of questions about cosmic time. I have put them here because they are really separate from the chapter.

Does the Lord come in every yuga-cycle?

No — once in a day of Brahmā. A kalpa, a day of Brahmā, has 1,000 yuga-cycles. Kṛṣṇa appears in the 28th cycle of Vaivasvata Manu, Rāmacandra in the 24th. SB 2.7 is the reference for these scheduled incarnations. So there is a very long time between one appearance and the next, and a very long time before He comes again. Which means we are very fortunate. He came recently.

What happens at the end of Brahmā's day?

Partial devastation. Night comes for Brahmā, as long as his day. The Garbhodaka ocean rises and floods the lower planetary systems — the three lower lokas and the seven below them. The upper four, up to Brahmaloka, are not flooded. At the end of the night the water goes down — rather like the Everglades here in Fort Lauderdale — and the forms of life appear again: the aquatics first, then the plants, and on through the species.
Not chemical evolution. Let me be clear. What happens again is the transmigration of the soul through the lower species up to the higher ones. It is not one species turning into another. From the outside the sequence looks similar. The mechanism is completely different.

And at the end of Brahmā's life?

Total annihilation. Everything is withdrawn, Brahmā included. Gross into subtle, subtle into subtler, back to the unmanifested pradhāna state — as if a vacuum cleaner had been applied to the whole thing. It stays that way until time, which moves in cycles, brings the next creation round again. The arithmetic on his lifespan is easy enough to do in your head: a day, plus a night of the same length, times thirty, times a hundred years.

Where to read it properly. Two places in the Third Canto: Maitreya's description to Vidura, and Lord Kapila's description to Devahūti.

How are day and night defined for Brahmā?

Here I can only give part of an answer. Time is simply different for Brahmā than it is for us. Only yesterday I was listening to Prabhupāda describing Einstein's theory of relativity, and this is the same subject. Our day and night come from the relative movement of the Earth and the sun — the Earth turns on its axis. Brahmaloka is at the very top of the universal structure, and the Earth is below the sun, so you cannot simply transfer that explanation. What can be said is that in Vedic cosmology the sun also moves, on its wheel of time — kāla-cakra — and the sun's position in relation to Brahmaloka has something to do with day and night there. Beyond that: I don't have the answer to that.

Worth noticing as method. Three of these questions have exact answers with references. The fourth gets part of an answer and an honest stop. I would rather leave the gap where you can see it.
